<p> In the summer of 2026, I spend 3 months working at Imec in the <strong>CMST</strong><sup class="citation" contenteditable="false" data-reference="imec-cmst-group" data-locator=""><a href="#ref-cfc3949e-d1ac-4c52-991e-234b0105a090-1" aria-label="Reference 1" title="">[1]</a></sup><strong> </strong>group. The project that they were working on was active alignment of ball lenses. To put it into context a little bit first.</p>
<p>What is a chip? Fundamentally silicon is just really good clay for us to mold, in two important ways. We can mold it into most shapes that we can imagine and do it <em>really</em><sup class="citation explanation-marker" contenteditable="false" data-note="3e9c078a-050a-4665-923b-ecb2f58fffd1"><a href="#ref-cfc3949e-d1ac-4c52-991e-234b0105a090-note-1" aria-label="Explanation 1">*</a></sup> accurately. This is because it reacts a certain way to some chemicals and wavelengths, which allows us to specify what parts to keep and what parts to remove. The second reason for it being a good material is that, when silicon takes a specific shape and gets electrocuted it starts to do math.<sup class="citation" contenteditable="false" data-reference="imec-chip-fabrication" data-locator="Photoresist coating; Lithography; Etch; Ion implantation"><a href="#ref-cfc3949e-d1ac-4c52-991e-234b0105a090-2" aria-label="Reference 2, Photoresist coating; Lithography; Etch; Ion implantation" title="Photoresist coating; Lithography; Etch; Ion implantation">[2]</a></sup></p>
<p>Now aside from that specific shape (the transistor) we can do so much more with it. The industry of photonics is about carving shapes for light. We can carve a road for light, that splits into 2 and merges again, and when electrocuted, one of the split roads becomes harder for light to propagate. Resulting in interference of light controlled by electricity.<sup class="citation" contenteditable="false" data-reference="imec-optical-modulation" data-locator="p. 60, III. Modulator integration"><a href="#ref-cfc3949e-d1ac-4c52-991e-234b0105a090-3" aria-label="Reference 3, p. 60, III. Modulator integration" title="p. 60, III. Modulator integration">[3]</a></sup> Everything light related, except for the producing light, can be done by silicon. Now, if silicon could produce lasers, then it really would have been the perfect material, but alas it is not so. Thus, a large and chunky laser has to added to our delicate chip, somehow.</p>
<p>There are many ways of doing this; either wire it in from the outside or place a &quot;small&quot; laser on top of the silicon.<sup class="citation" contenteditable="false" data-reference="imec-hybrid-lasers" data-locator="External sources and high-precision hybrid laser integration"><a href="#ref-cfc3949e-d1ac-4c52-991e-234b0105a090-4" aria-label="Reference 4, External sources and high-precision hybrid laser integration" title="External sources and high-precision hybrid laser integration">[4]</a></sup> The former has some very obvious downsides. The latter has the issue that, if you even slightly misplace the laser relative to the chip, you get nothing. The laser beam misses the intended target by one beam width, and you have wasted a $250 laser, a research chip<sup class="citation explanation-marker" contenteditable="false" data-note="imec-component-cost-context"><a href="#ref-cfc3949e-d1ac-4c52-991e-234b0105a090-note-2" aria-label="Explanation 2">**</a></sup> and hours of machine time.</p>
<p>A possible solution is to intentionally leave a gap between laser and waveguide. Placing <a href="#media-image/assets/evidence/imec/04-real-focus-study-sharp.png">two lenses</a> in the between and adjust their positions to find and focus the laser into the waveguide. Which was my job, how do you get the lenses there? And then how do you optimize for light?</p>
<p><a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B5ln2s0bnAY">How I did it</a> exactly I cannot say, I think, it would be bad manners to do so, but it was very well received and is robust enough that it is still working, even after I transferred it to a PhD working on this project.</p>
