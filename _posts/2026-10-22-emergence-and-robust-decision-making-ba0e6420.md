<p>People keep asking me what I want to study next, and I keep telling them this long story about Conway, AI, the brain and robustness. So, for practice, I will write it down here, to keep the story consistent.</p>
<p>All laws of physics obey locality<sup class="citation explanation-marker" contenteditable="false" data-note="72c15a8d-8a13-41da-b3d0-a6911ee30b60"><a href="#ref-ba0e6420-8ec0-44b5-8d92-d649a70bad96-note-1" aria-label="Explanation 1">*</a></sup> . What that means is that nothing can affect anything that is far away. It can only do so through propagation. The act of affecting your neighbors and those neighbors affecting their neighbors, etc. And yet, it seems that we can see far away, we can call people and even control things at a distance in very complex ways. But these are emergent properties of the local rules. I am very interested in emergence. How an ant knows how to find food is emergent behaviour, in the same way that the Sun shining is emergent. </p>
<p>Decision-making is especially emergent, and in the computer age we have been trying to automate that. Decision makers of the first kind started in Bletchley Park or Bell Labs, where with the birth of the Unix operating system<sup class="citation" contenteditable="false" data-reference="ritchie-unix-evolution" data-locator="Origins of Unix; 1969"><a href="#ref-ba0e6420-8ec0-44b5-8d92-d649a70bad96-2" aria-label="Reference 2, Origins of Unix; 1969" title="Origins of Unix; 1969">[2]</a></sup> we could encode decisionmakers as software, meaning <a href="#media-image/assets/evidence/game-of-life/py4e-multiway.webp">decision trees</a><sup class="citation" contenteditable="false" data-reference="severance-multiway" data-locator="Slide 14: code beside its decision branches"><a href="#ref-ba0e6420-8ec0-44b5-8d92-d649a70bad96-12" aria-label="Reference 12, Slide 14: code beside its decision branches" title="Slide 14: code beside its decision branches">[12]</a></sup>. All code is Turing-complete and therefore can be written as a decision tree, i.e., if or else, followed by if or else statements<sup class="citation explanation-marker" contenteditable="false" data-note="8f8fc76e-b29e-46d3-b452-5f6ae39c1fc8"><a href="#ref-ba0e6420-8ec0-44b5-8d92-d649a70bad96-note-2" aria-label="Explanation 2">**</a></sup> . We call this &quot;programming&quot; now.</p>
<p>The second kind, which was discovered a decade ago, is the neural network. Which by storing decision-making inside a black box and adjusting that black box a little every time it makes a mistake, we have made it make decisions. Now it seems that the second kind is &quot;solved&quot;. We just got to extract all of its emergent behaviour, which will still take some time and a lot of breakthroughs.<sup class="citation explanation-marker" contenteditable="false" data-note="ai-solved-scaling-view"><a href="#ref-ba0e6420-8ec0-44b5-8d92-d649a70bad96-note-3" aria-label="Explanation 3">***</a></sup></p>
<p>Another kind of decision-making model will obviously be the dynamical system. A decision-making model of the 3rd kind. Which is a system that evolves in discrete space (grids, cells, hypergraphs) and according to discrete time. To flesh it out, you can imagine Conway’s Game of Life<sup class="citation" contenteditable="false" data-reference="gardner-life-1970" data-locator="Game rules"><a href="#ref-ba0e6420-8ec0-44b5-8d92-d649a70bad96-4" aria-label="Reference 4, Game rules" title="Game rules">[4]</a></sup>. And if you imagine it with a chosen starting condition <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://sjaakiechen.github.io/personal-blog/assets/evidence/demos/game-of-life-sender-receiver/index.html?load=auto">like here</a>, you can have a reaction that goes on. By artificially introducing regions of input and output you can see how this thing could produce decision-making. Why would this be useful? Well, by adjusting the rules, layout of the grid and starting condition, you can reproduce any decision-making model of the first and second kind. (See <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xP5-iIeKXE8">Game of Life inside the Game of Life</a><sup class="citation" contenteditable="false" data-reference="bradbury-life-in-life-2012" data-locator="90-second visual demonstration"><a href="#ref-ba0e6420-8ec0-44b5-8d92-d649a70bad96-8" aria-label="Reference 8, 90-second visual demonstration" title="90-second visual demonstration">[8]</a></sup> which is a Turing-complete game<sup class="citation" contenteditable="false" data-reference="rendell-life-universality" data-locator="Turing-machine construction"><a href="#ref-ba0e6420-8ec0-44b5-8d92-d649a70bad96-5" aria-label="Reference 5, Turing-machine construction" title="Turing-machine construction">[5]</a></sup>)</p>
<p>But most importantly, nature has demonstrated that this type of system has potential, evident by animal brains. The problem with decision trees and neural networks is that they are not general at all.  You design them against one metric and that is it, whether the prediction of words<sup class="citation" contenteditable="false" data-reference="brown-language-models-2020" data-locator="Section 3.1, p. 11"><a href="#ref-ba0e6420-8ec0-44b5-8d92-d649a70bad96-9" aria-label="Reference 9, Section 3.1, p. 11" title="Section 3.1, p. 11">[9]</a></sup><sup class="citation" contenteditable="false" data-reference="vaswani-transformer-2017" data-locator="Section 3, pp. 2–3; Figure 1"><a href="#ref-ba0e6420-8ec0-44b5-8d92-d649a70bad96-10" aria-label="Reference 10, Section 3, pp. 2–3; Figure 1" title="Section 3, pp. 2–3; Figure 1">[10]</a></sup> or the response to a chess move<sup class="citation" contenteditable="false" data-reference="silver-alphazero-2017" data-locator="Pages 2–3: move probabilities, values and search"><a href="#ref-ba0e6420-8ec0-44b5-8d92-d649a70bad96-11" aria-label="Reference 11, Pages 2–3: move probabilities, values and search" title="Pages 2–3: move probabilities, values and search">[11]</a></sup>. Of course, they are much more cost-effective at the given task, but for general purpose decision-making we need to experiment on the third kind. As robustness is the only important metric.<br></p>
<p>A demonstration of this is <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://sjaakiechen.github.io/personal-blog/assets/evidence/demos/joy-checkpoint-5850-live/index.html?load=auto">my reproduction</a> of a paper<sup class="citation" contenteditable="false" data-reference="growing-nca-2020" data-locator="Cellular Automaton rule; Experiment 3"><a href="#ref-ba0e6420-8ec0-44b5-8d92-d649a70bad96-6" aria-label="Reference 6, Cellular Automaton rule; Experiment 3" title="Cellular Automaton rule; Experiment 3">[6]</a></sup>, discovering that by finding the right local rules, a Game of Life system can regenerate itself from destruction. (Without knowing anything other than: given the neighbor is alive or not, should I be alive or not)</p>
<p>The local rule was a neural net, which in turn is just code i.e a 1st kind decision maker. I found a local rule that works for my favorite emoji, by using a repurposed old laptop and training on it for 2 weeks.</p>
